A proposal for Golden Gate Park · Winter 2026

Crossover-Over

Giving the middle of the park back its quiet.

On a still night this winter, stand on the west shore of Blue Heron Lake and listen. The great horned owls that nest on Strawberry Hill are courting, calling back and forth across the water. Under them, all night long, runs Highway 1.

71,000 vehicles a day cross the park on Crossover Drive, by Caltrans' 2024 count. About 5,200 of them come through in the evening peak hour.

Crossover Drive is a six-lane state highway that WPA crews built between 1936 and 1940 to carry traffic to the new Golden Gate Bridge. It cuts the park at its narrow waist, from Lincoln Way at 19th Avenue to Fulton Street at Park Presidio. It fills about a hundred acres of the park with the sound of tires. Along Fulton it walls off six blocks of the Inner Richmond from the park they face.

This is a plan to get that land back, in four steps. Each one is worth doing even if the next never happens. None of them takes a lane away from anyone.

Chapter I

The road through the woods

Caltrans controls Crossover Drive. Every other road in the park belongs to Rec & Park. A separate two-lane spur, also called Crossover Drive, runs out to Fulton at 25th Avenue.

The highway comes north up 19th Avenue, enters the park at Lincoln Way and climbs onto a bench cut into the flank of Strawberry Hill. It crosses over the JFK Promenade on a WPA bridge, then swings east as the Park Presidio Bypass and runs along the inside of Fulton Street until it leaves the park at Park Presidio Boulevard.

Chapter II

What you hear

The WHO sets 55 dB as the daytime level outdoors where serious annoyance begins. US highway rules use 57 dB for land "where serenity and quiet are of extraordinary significance" and 67 dB for parks generally.

Standing on the lake shore, about 200 feet from the nearest lane, the highway runs around 60 decibels, about the level of a conversation at arm's length. It drops below 55 only 600 feet out. Inside that line sit Blue Heron Lake's west shore, Strawberry Hill, Rainbow Falls, Lloyd Lake and the woods along Transverse Drive.

Senzaki and colleagues played recorded traffic to 78 wild owls in Japan. At 40 dB the owls found 17% fewer prey; at 80 dB, 89% fewer. The effect reached more than 120 m from a road.

Owls hunt by ear. In a 2016 field study, traffic noise as quiet as 40 dB cut how often wild owls found their prey. The Strawberry Hill nest sits inside the 55 to 60 dB wash.

Chapter III

Where you can't get in

Fulton Street is the park's front porch for the Richmond. From Park Presidio to 21st Avenue, the highway runs right behind it. For about 2,900 feet of road there is no way across: no crosswalk, no signal, no underpass.

SF Planning's 2018 study of the park's edges found that 28% of Fulton's 54 intersections have no crosswalk, and that people "enter the park wherever they desire" anyway.

From the corner of Fulton and 18th Avenue, JFK Drive is 145 metres away. On foot it is 652 metres, because you have to walk to Park Presidio or around to the JFK underpass first. Six blocks have walks more than two and a half times the straight-line distance. Everywhere else along Fulton, people walk straight in.

The 22nd Avenue entrance works because of a 2023 city project: a signalized crossing and a newly paved path. It is the cheapest precedent on this exact edge.

Between the highway and Fulton lies a strip of park about 100 metres deep. Counting the wedges cut off by the 25th Avenue spur, it covers 19 acres, more than Presidio Tunnel Tops. It has trees and grass and nowhere to go.

Chapter IV

The lay of the land

Walk the road in lidar and its shape is plain. North of MLK the highway runs on a shelf cut into Strawberry Hill. The ground climbs 30 to 40 feet to the lake on one side and falls 20 to 40 feet to Transverse Drive on the other. Along Fulton it is nearly level, a few feet above the park to the south.

The lake surface sits at about 285 ft and Transverse Drive at about 207 ft, a drop of 75 to 80 feet. Strawberry Hill's summit is about 429 ft.

That shelf is good news. A bridge can land on the hillside at grade. A lid can lean against the hill on one side and be built up as a planted slope on the other. Along Fulton, where the road sits a little high, the ground suits an underpass.

Chapter V

Four ways to give it back

The phases run cheapest first. Costs are planning ranges in 2026 dollars and include design, Caltrans oversight and contingency. At this stage the real figure could land half as high or twice as high.

The first stepQuiet the road

At city speeds most of a highway's noise comes from tires on pavement. Caltrans' own test sections show rubberized open-graded asphalt keeps its quieting longest. Lowering the posted limit from 35 to 30 miles an hour quiets the road by another 1.7 decibels. Together that is about four decibels, enough to pull the 55 dB line in by a third.

If Caltrans already has this stretch on its resurfacing schedule, the extra cost is the difference in asphalt mix.

The second stepThree ways across

The first two crossings open the Fulton edge. The third lets you walk from the lake straight into the western woods.

Fulton & 17th Avenue

The Fulton Grotto

A 16-foot-wide stone-lined underpass beneath the bypass. The floor meets the park at grade on the south side, and a gentle ramp climbs to Fulton. The park has done this before: the 1889 Alvord Lake Bridge, the first reinforced-concrete bridge in the country, is a grotto under a park road.

Fulton & 19th Avenue

Prayer Book Cross Bridge

A walking bridge from the stranded strip to the hill below the Prayer Book Cross. The south end lands on the hillside at grade. On the Fulton side a long planted ramp rises along the strip itself, which finally gives that land a purpose.

Strawberry Hill

Strawberry Hill Bridge

A bridge from the lake's shore road to the Transverse Drive trail. It meets the hill at grade on the east. To the west it becomes a trail that winds down through the trees to Transverse. It saves little distance, since MLK is close, but you never have to wait at a six-lane intersection.

The Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing over US-101 cost about $114M. One foundation gave $25M, and more than $34M came from philanthropy in all. Palo Alto's Adobe Creek bike bridge over 101 cost $23.7M.

The third stepStrawberry Hill Lid

A planted roof, 787 feet long, over both directions of traffic between MLK and JFK. At that length it needs no ventilation fans and no control room. On the lake side it leans into the hill; on the west it falls away as a meadow slope toward Transverse Drive. It quiets about 15 acres, and it puts 787 feet of hillside where you can walk from the lake straight into the woods.

Of every 787-foot stretch of the road tested, this one quiets the most land.

The last stepThe Crossover Tunnel

A cut-and-cover tunnel about 3,900 feet long, from just north of the MLK signal to just west of Fulton and Park Presidio. It is built half at a time on the road's own line, so traffic keeps moving throughout. The JFK Promenade keeps passing beneath it through a longer grotto. Every lane stays and every trip still gets through, and the park gets its middle back.

A cheaper version, the String of Lids, uses four 787-ft roofs with short gaps. It quiets about two-thirds as much land for about 60% of the cost, if a fire-safety review accepts the gaps.

A tunnel this long needs fans, fire systems and someone watching it, about $4–6M a year. Its nearest relative is the Presidio Parkway, whose cut-and-cover tunnels opened in 2015 and carry Tunnel Tops on their roof.

Chapter VII

What it costs, and what it's worth

Quiet doesn't trade on a market, so any dollar figure for it rests on assumptions. This ledger shows them so you can change them. The starting values are cautious. The park averages about 23,600 visits per acre a year, and the ledger assumes half that. It values a quieter visit at $4, below the Army Corps' 2025 range of $5–15 for a day of general recreation.

Quieting the road pays for itself many times over at almost any setting, so it should simply happen. The crossings come close to paying their way in walking time and new visits, and their real case is the Richmond's front door, which a ledger can't price. The lid and the tunnel don't pass a conventional benefit-cost test, and this proposal won't pretend they do.

Their case is the last column. Seventy-odd acres of quiet parkland in the middle of the city can't be bought at any price, and the tunnel costs about what that land would if you could. Tunnel Tops, Klyde Warren and the Annenberg crossing were all built on that argument. It is an argument for a gift, or for a city that plans a hundred years ahead.

Chapter VIII

How it gets built, and who might give

Any work over, under or on Crossover is work on a state highway inside a National Register historic district. So Caltrans owns the road, Rec & Park owns the park, and the County Transportation Authority is the natural sponsor for highway projects.

  1. A cooperative agreement and project study with Caltrans. The quiet pavement and the three crossings can share one study. Six to twelve months.
  2. Environmental clearance. California exempts public walking and biking projects from CEQA review through January 1, 2030 (Public Resources Code §21080.25). The crossings should be designed to qualify and approved before that date. The lid and tunnel need full environmental review.
  3. Historic review. Treat the WPA stonework and the JFK bridge as part of the historic park. A grotto and a planted bridge fit the park's language better than a steel truss.
  4. Design and a Caltrans encroachment permit, including how to keep 71,000 cars a day moving during construction.
  5. Construction. The crossings are short jobs done with overnight closures. A lid or tunnel goes in half at a time.
In April 2026 a Richmond resident asked SFCTA to study putting Park Presidio Boulevard underground, from the park to the Presidio, on the Presidio Parkway model. That is the same road one mile north. A single study of Route 1 from Lincoln Way to the Presidio is a stronger ask than either half alone.

The fastest real start is a $1–2M feasibility study for the first two steps, paid for privately and handed to SFCTA and Rec & Park. That is how Tunnel Tops and the Annenberg crossing began: donors paid for the plans, and the plans made the public money easy to commit.

San Francisco has a long habit of park gifts. In November 2026 the city received the largest single gift in its history, $100 million for parks, and the mayor asked others to follow. Each step here suits a different kind of giver.

Quiet the road · $3–8M

Public. A letter from Rec & Park and the District 1 and 7 supervisors to Caltrans, plus the difference in asphalt.

Three crossings · $49–86M

One family or a small circle, or a line in the next round of the city's parks gift. Three things to name.

The lid · $140–240M

A campaign on the Tunnel Tops model, with a lead gift that names the hill.

The tunnel · $0.9–1.6B

State and federal highway money with a philanthropic match, or a city bond. The gift people would still be walking on in 2126.

Chapter IX

Go and listen

Great horned owls hoot most in December and January. Go to the west shore of Blue Heron Lake at dusk on a still evening and listen for the low call and the answer. Then walk down toward Transverse Drive and notice where the highway takes over.

The sound map is a model, and an afternoon with a sound meter can check it. A calibrated phone app with an external microphone is enough.

  • Walk a line straight out from the road at the Strawberry Hill Bridge site, and another south from Fulton at 17th Avenue.
  • Record ten minutes at 50, 100, 200, 400 and 800 feet from the nearest lane, about five feet off the ground.
  • Do it at 8 am, 1 pm and 5 pm on a calm weekday. Note the wind, planes and mowers.
  • Repeat early on a Sunday to hear the park on its own.
